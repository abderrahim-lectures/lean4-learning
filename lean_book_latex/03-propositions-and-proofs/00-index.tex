\chapter{Chapter 3: Propositions as types, and basic proofs}\label{sec:03-propositions-and-proofs:00-index:1}

\begin{learningobjectives}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Read \texttt{Prop} as the type of statements and a proof as an ordinary term.
\item
  State the introduction/elimination rules of natural deduction for \texttt{∧}/\texttt{∨}/\texttt{¬}/\texttt{→}.
\item
  Write and prove \texttt{theorem}/\texttt{lemma}s directly as terms.
\item
  Reason about \texttt{∀}/\texttt{∃} and equality via the anonymous constructor and \texttt{rfl}.
\end{itemize}
\end{learningobjectives}


Each section again answers the question the previous one forces.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Chapters 1 and 2 built terms and types for numbers, points, and soon-to-be groups. But what is a \emph{proof}, as a piece of Lean data?} (\hyperref[sec:03-propositions-and-proofs:01-prop:1]{Section 1}) The Curry--Howard correspondence. A proposition is a type, \texttt{Prop}, and a proof is simply a term of that type, the same terms-and-types machinery already in place, turned on statements instead of on numbers.
\item
  \textbf{That correspondence assumes ``propositional logic'' and ``natural deduction'' are already familiar. Are they?} (\hyperref[sec:03-propositions-and-proofs:02-logic-recap:1]{Section 2}) A self-contained, Lean-free recap of standard logic, fixing exactly what the ``Logic'' column of the table in Section 1 refers to, so the translation into types has something precise to translate.
\item
  \textbf{Now that a proposition is a type, how does one actually \emph{state} and \emph{name} a proof, the way ordinary mathematics names a theorem?} (\hyperref[sec:03-propositions-and-proofs:03-theorem-lemma:1]{Section 3}) \texttt{theorem} and \texttt{lemma} are syntactically identical to \texttt{def}, since a proof is a term like any other.
\item
  \textbf{The most basic rule of natural deduction, \(\Rightarrow\)-intro, was already recognizable as \texttt{fun (hp : P) => ...}. Is that resemblance exact, or only suggestive?} (\hyperref[sec:03-propositions-and-proofs:04-implication:1]{Section 4}) Exact. Implication \emph{is} the function type, and modus ponens \emph{is} function application, with nothing lost in the translation.
\item
  \textbf{Implication is one connective. What about the rest, \texttt{∧}, \texttt{∨}, \texttt{¬}?} (\hyperref[sec:03-propositions-and-proofs:05-and-or-not:1]{Section 5}) Each gets its own type former (product, sum, function-to-\texttt{False}), completing the dictionary that Section 1 only started.
\item
  \textbf{Every connective so far has been about \emph{fixed} propositions \texttt{P}, \texttt{Q}. What about a statement varying over every \texttt{n}, or asserting that \emph{some} witness exists?} (\hyperref[sec:03-propositions-and-proofs:06-quantifiers:1]{Section 6}) \texttt{∀} and \texttt{∃} are exactly the Π- and Σ-types of Chapter 1, specialized to a family landing in \texttt{Prop} instead of \texttt{Type}. Dependent types were never optional scaffolding, and here is where they pay off for logic itself.
\item
  \textbf{One relation has been used silently in every section so far, \texttt{rfl}, \texttt{=}, without ever being examined on its own terms. What \emph{is} it, precisely, and what can be done with it?} (\hyperref[sec:03-propositions-and-proofs:07-equality:1]{Section 7}) Equality reasoning covers reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, and substitution, the tools every later proof in this book leans on without comment.
\end{enumerate}

By the last section, ``propositions are types, proofs are terms'' has gone from a one-line slogan to a working toolkit. Every connective and quantifier in ordinary mathematical writing now has a precise Lean counterpart, ready for the tactics of Chapter 4 to build automatically instead of by hand.
