\chapter{Chapter 8: Rings --- adding a second operation}\label{sec:08-rings:00-index:1}

Chapter 7 finished everything Part II had to say about a single operation. A ring is what happens once a second operation is added alongside it, and each section below follows from that one change:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{What, precisely, changes when a second operation is layered onto a group?} (\hyperref[sec:08-rings:01-definition:1]{Section 1}) The additive side must now be commutative, and a compatible multiplication is added, tied to addition by the distributive laws --- stated first as ordinary axioms (R1)--(R4), the way Chapter 6 opened with the group axioms before any Lean.
\item
  \textbf{Axiom (R1) demands \emph{commutative} addition --- but Chapter 6's \texttt{Group} said nothing about commutativity. Where does that requirement come from in Lean?} (\hyperref[sec:08-rings:02-comm-group:1]{Section 2}) \texttt{CommGroup}, a small \texttt{extends Group G} with one extra field, \texttt{comm}. This is the piece \texttt{Ring} will bundle as its additive part.
\item
  \textbf{With that piece in hand, what does the full \texttt{Ring} structure look like?} (\hyperref[sec:08-rings:03-ring:1]{Section 3}) \texttt{CommGroup} supplies (R1) as a single nested field, and the remaining axioms (R2)--(R4) are added directly: multiplication's associativity, its identity, and the two distributive laws.
\item
  \textbf{Is the definition non-vacuous?} (\hyperref[sec:08-rings:04-integers-example:1]{Section 4}) Yes --- \((\mathbb{Z}, +, \times, 0, 1)\), reusing Chapter 6's \texttt{intGroup} for the additive part and citing one core-library lemma per remaining axiom, exactly the pattern Chapter 6 used for \texttt{Group}.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{intRing} is commutative and infinite --- does every ring axiom have to be proved this way, one library citation at a time?} (\hyperref[sec:08-rings:05-finite-ring-example:1]{Section 5}) Not when the carrier is finite: \(\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}\)'s axioms are decidable statements about only finitely many elements, so \texttt{decide} can check them by brute enumeration instead.
\item
  \textbf{How is this structure's data actually used, now that it nests a whole \texttt{CommGroup} inside it?} (\hyperref[sec:08-rings:06-accessing-fields:1]{Section 6}) By ordinary field projection, chained one level deeper than Chapter 6 --- \texttt{Rg.addGrp.op} rather than a single \texttt{Grp.op}.
\item
  \textbf{\texttt{intRing} is commutative --- does anything in \texttt{Ring}'s definition actually forbid that, the way Chapter 6's permutations tested \texttt{Group} for hidden commutativity assumptions?} (\hyperref[sec:08-rings:07-matrices:1]{Section 7}) No, and \(2\times 2\) integer matrices prove it directly: a concrete, genuinely noncommutative ring, built and verified field by field with no shortcuts available.
\end{enumerate}

By the end of the chapter, \texttt{Ring} has the same shape \texttt{Group} did at the end of Chapter 6: a precise definition, both a commutative and a noncommutative witness that it is satisfiable, and --- as Chapter 9 takes up next --- theorems still waiting to be proved generically rather than per carrier.
