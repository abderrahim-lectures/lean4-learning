\section{What forces the definition}\label{sec:11-modules:00-index:2}

A \texttt{Ring} (Chapter 9) already generalizes a field by dropping division. The vector-space axioms make sense verbatim under the same move: an abelian group \(M\) equipped with a scalar action \(R \times M \to M\) satisfying the same four distributivity/associativity/unit laws, with ``field'' weakened to ``ring.'' This is the \textbf{module} (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:01-definition:1]{Section 1}), the subject of the whole chapter.

A definition stated on paper is not yet something Lean can check proofs against. It is written down as a \texttt{structure} the same ``data, then axioms'' way already used for \texttt{Group} and \texttt{Ring}, with one difference: the ring \(R\) a module is defined \emph{over} enters as an explicit argument to \texttt{Module}, not as bundled data, because a module is always a module over some already-fixed ring (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:02-translating-into-lean:1]{Section 2}).

The definition is non-vacuous: every abelian group is a \(\mathbb{Z}\)-module, and the scalar action is \emph{forced}, not chosen. The initiality of \(\mathbb{Z}\) itself as a ring pins down \(n \cdot m\) uniquely by induction, so ``abelian group'' and ``\(\mathbb{Z}\)-module'' turn out to be the same notion, seen from two directions (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:03-z-module-example:1]{Section 3}).

Given one module, the subsets that inherit the same structure are the \textbf{submodules} (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:04-submodules:1]{Section 4}), the module analogue of a subgroup: a subset closed under addition, containing \(0\), and closed under the scalar action, encoded the same way every ``subobject of \(X\)'' has been encoded since Chapter 3, a membership predicate bundled with closure proofs, rather than a \texttt{Set X} wrapped separately.

Given a module, the structure-preserving maps \emph{between} two of them are \textbf{linear maps} (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:05-linear-maps:1]{Section 5}), additive and compatible with the scalar action on both sides. This supplies the morphisms of the category \(R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}\), completing the objects-and-morphisms picture Chapter 1 first set up for \texttt{Type} itself.

With objects and morphisms both available, new modules are built out of old ones by the \textbf{direct sum} \(M \oplus N\) (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:06-direct-sums:1]{Section 6}), componentwise structure on the product \(M \times N\), verified field by field with \texttt{congr 1}, and equipped with projection maps that are themselves instances of the \texttt{LinearMap} of Section 5, nothing new needed to state them, only what is already on the table.

By the last section, ``module'' has gone from a paper generalization of ``vector space'' to a working Lean \texttt{structure}, complete with a canonical example, its subobjects, its morphisms, and one way to build new modules from old. Chapter 12 puts this machinery to its intended use: a representation of a quiver is exactly a module over its path algebra, which is why this chapter comes immediately before it.
