\chapter{Chapter 11: Modules over a ring}\label{sec:11-modules:00-index:1}

\begin{learningobjectives}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Translate the module axioms into a Lean \texttt{structure} parameterized by an explicit ring.
\item
  Build a \texttt{Submodule} as a membership predicate with closure proofs.
\item
  Define a \texttt{LinearMap} and check its two defining properties.
\item
  Assemble a direct sum of modules field by field via \texttt{congr 1}.
\end{itemize}
\end{learningobjectives}


Each section below answers the question the previous one forces.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{A \texttt{Ring}, from Chapter 9, already generalizes a field by dropping division. What happens to a \emph{vector space} under the same move?} (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:01-definition:1]{Section 1}) The vector-space axioms make sense verbatim over an arbitrary ring, not just a field. An abelian group \(M\) equipped with a scalar action \(R \times M \to M\) satisfying the same four distributivity/associativity/unit laws. This is the \textbf{module}, and it is the subject of the whole chapter.
\item
  \textbf{Stated on paper, this definition is not yet something Lean can check proofs against. How is it written down as a \texttt{structure}?} (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:02-translating-into-lean:1]{Section 2}) Exactly the same ``data, then axioms'' translation already used for \texttt{Group} and \texttt{Ring}, with one difference, the ring \(R\) a module is defined \emph{over} enters as an explicit argument to \texttt{Module}, not as bundled data, because a module is always a module over some already-fixed ring.
\item
  \textbf{Is this definition merely formally sound, or does something already familiar satisfy it?} (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:03-z-module-example:1]{Section 3}) Every abelian group is a \(\mathbb{Z}\)-module, and moreover the scalar action is \emph{forced}, not chosen. The initiality of \(\mathbb{Z}\) itself as a ring pins down \(n \cdot m\) uniquely by induction. ``Abelian group'' and ``\(\mathbb{Z}\)-module'' turn out to be the same notion, seen from two directions.
\item
  \textbf{Given one module, which of its subsets inherit the same structure?} (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:04-submodules:1]{Section 4}) A \textbf{submodule}, the module analogue of a subgroup, a subset closed under addition, containing \(0\), and closed under the scalar action, encoded in Lean the same way every ``subobject of \(X\)'' has been encoded since Chapter 3: a membership predicate bundled with closure proofs, rather than a \texttt{Set X} wrapped separately.
\item
  \textbf{Given a module, what are the structure-preserving maps \emph{between} two of them?} (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:05-linear-maps:1]{Section 5}) A \textbf{linear map}, additive, and compatible with the scalar action on both sides. This supplies the morphisms of the category \(R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}\), completing the objects-and-morphisms picture Chapter 1 first set up for \texttt{Type} itself.
\item
  \textbf{With objects and morphisms both available, how are new modules built out of old ones?} (\hyperref[sec:11-modules:06-direct-sums:1]{Section 6}) The \textbf{direct sum} \(M \oplus N\), componentwise structure on the product \(M \times N\), verified field by field with \texttt{congr 1}, and equipped with projection maps that are themselves instances of the \texttt{LinearMap} of Section 5, nothing new is needed to state them, only what is already on the table.
\end{enumerate}

By the last section, ``module'' has gone from a paper generalization of ``vector space'' to a working Lean \texttt{structure}, complete with a canonical example, its subobjects, its morphisms, and one way to build new modules from old. Chapter 12 puts this machinery to its intended use, a representation of a quiver is exactly a module over its path algebra, which is why this chapter comes immediately before it.
