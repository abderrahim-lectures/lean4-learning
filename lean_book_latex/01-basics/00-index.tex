\section{What Lean demands of a proof}\label{sec:01-basics:00-index:2}

A mathematician writes ``the square root of 2 is irrational'' and sketches a few sentences. Lean demands a term whose type \emph{is} the statement, with every gap filled. Here is the contrast on three statements, from trivial to open, showing what ``formalized'' means before any machinery is learned.

\textbf{Example 1. Reflexivity.} ``\(0 = 0\).''

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Informal proof.} ``Immediate.''
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Lean formalization.}

\begin{lstlisting}
theorem zero_eq_zero : (0 : Nat) = 0 := rfl
-- rfl closes any goal of the form a = a by definition
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}

The gap is small but already visible: even the trivial case needs a named declaration and a tactic. A human can leave it implicit; Lean cannot.

\textbf{Example 2. Symmetry of equality.} ``If \(a = b\), then \(b = a\).''

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Informal proof.} ``By symmetry of equality.''
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Lean formalization.}

\begin{lstlisting}
theorem symm_example {a b : Nat} (h : a = b) : b = a := h.symm
-- h.symm is a built-in method on equality proofs,
-- directly reflecting the symmetry axiom of Leibniz equality
\end{lstlisting}
\end{quote}

A single word in the informal version (``symmetry'') becomes a term-level method call. The \emph{structure} is the same --- hypothesis in, conclusion out --- but the gap between ``by symmetry'' and \texttt{h.symm} is exactly the gap this book teaches to close.

\textbf{Example 3. An open problem.} ``Every even number greater than 2 is the sum of two primes.'' (Goldbach's conjecture)

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Informal proof.} ``Verified up to \(4 \times 10^{18}\), but unproven.''
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Lean formalization.} No term of type \texttt{∀ n, n > 2 → Even n → ∃ p q, Prime p ∧ Prime q ∧ n = p + q} exists in any library. Lean cannot prove it because mathematics has not proved it. A proof assistant does not guess; it checks. When a statement is open, Lean has nothing to offer, and the honest answer is a \texttt{sorry} or an absent file.
\end{quote}

These three examples --- closed-but-trivial, closed-but-needing-structure, and open --- set the range. Every proof in this book lives somewhere on that line. The rest of the chapters build the vocabulary to move from the first kind to the second, and to recognize the third for what it is.

\section{What forces the chapter}\label{sec:01-basics:00-index:3}

Ordinary languages catch a type error, if at all, at the moment the bad line runs, possibly months after it shipped, on a rarely-hit branch. What would it take to catch it earlier, for every possible input at once, rather than one run at a time? Reading the expression once and deciding ``what kind of thing is this?'' before ever running it, exactly what \texttt{\#check} does and \texttt{\#eval} does not. \hyperref[sec:01-basics:01-everything-has-a-type:1]{Section 1} draws that line, \texttt{\#check} a static guarantee, \texttt{\#eval} a one-off fact, the foundation everything else in this book stands on.

Types checked this strictly still need to be built, and reused, without rewriting a definition once per type. \texttt{def} and \texttt{let} are the two ways to name a term; implicit arguments (\texttt{\{α : Type\}}) are how a single definition like \texttt{identity} serves \emph{every} type at once, without being rewritten per type. \hyperref[sec:01-basics:02-def-let-implicit:1]{Section 2} covers this vocabulary, no new machinery yet, just what is needed to read and write ordinary Lean definitions fluently.

Is a type always fixed in advance, the way \texttt{Nat → Nat} never changes its output type? No, and this is the one feature separating a proof assistant from an ordinary typed language: a type can depend on a \emph{value}, not just another type. \texttt{Fin n} is a different type for each \texttt{n}, and the \emph{return type} of a function can change depending on which argument it receives, letting a signature say ``these two lists must have the same length'' and have Lean enforce it, rather than merely hope for it. \hyperref[sec:01-basics:03-dependent-types:1]{Section 3} works this out concretely.

That single feature, a type depending on a value, is the hook the next chapter picks up directly. \texttt{Fin}/\texttt{Vec} are dependent types encountered concretely here; naming the vocabulary behind them precisely, and the formal system they are instances of, is the job of \hyperref[sec:02-terminology-and-coc:00-index:1]{Chapter 2}, not of this chapter.
