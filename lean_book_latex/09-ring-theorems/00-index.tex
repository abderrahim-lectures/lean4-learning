\chapter{Chapter 9: Ring examples and basic theorems}\label{sec:09-ring-theorems:00-index:1}

\begin{learningobjectives}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Derive a ring fact that is \emph{not} an axiom (e.g.~\(a\cdot 0=0\)) from the axioms that are.
\item
  Spot when a ``mirror'' proof (\texttt{left\_\allowbreak{}distrib} vs.~\texttt{right\_\allowbreak{}distrib}) can be copied line-by-line rather than re-derived.
\item
  Reduce a ``compute this'' goal to a ``verify this satisfies the characterizing property'' goal via a previously-proved uniqueness lemma.
\end{itemize}
\end{learningobjectives}


As in Chapter 7, the point here is the search process for each proof, not just the final term. Ring proofs are a good test of that process, because the goals become visually noisy fast (nested \texttt{addGrp.toGroup.op} everywhere). The skill under development is avoiding getting lost in that noise, and instead tracking which algebraic fact is one step away from use.

Chapter 8 built rings one concrete carrier at a time, \texttt{Int}, \texttt{Fin 3}, \(2\times 2\) matrices, reverifying the same axioms by hand for each. Chapter 7 already showed the way out of that repetition for groups. This chapter repeats it for rings, and each section follows from the last.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{How is a theorem about ``every ring'' even stated, mirroring what Chapter 7 did for groups?} (\hyperref[sec:09-ring-theorems:01-setup:1]{Section 1}) Fix an arbitrary \texttt{Rg : Ring R} with \texttt{variable}, exactly as Chapter 7 fixed an arbitrary \texttt{Grp : Group G}, with the added complication that qualified field names now nest two structures deep (\texttt{Rg.addGrp.toGroup.inv}), so a mental dictionary back to ordinary \texttt{+}/\texttt{×}/\texttt{0}/\texttt{1} notation is worth keeping close at hand.
\item
  \textbf{What is the first fact worth proving this way, given that the axioms of \texttt{Ring} say nothing directly about \texttt{0} and multiplication?} (\hyperref[sec:09-ring-theorems:02-theorem-1:1]{Section 2}) \(a \cdot 0 = 0\), not an axiom, but a consequence of distributivity plus the additive cancellation Chapter 7 already established, recognized by rewriting \(0\) as \(0+0\) and treating the resulting equation as the purely group-theoretic fact ``an element equal to its own double is zero.''
\item
  \textbf{Does the same reasoning extend to a genuinely new claim, the sign rule \((-1)\cdot a = -a\)?} (\hyperref[sec:09-ring-theorems:03-theorem-2:1]{Section 3}) Yes, by reusing \texttt{left\_\allowbreak{}inverse\_\allowbreak{}unique} from Chapter 7 directly: showing \((-1)\cdot
  a\) satisfies the \emph{characterizing} equation for \(-a\) is easier than computing \(-a\) from scratch, and the proof needs a mirror image of Theorem 1 (\texttt{right\_\allowbreak{}distrib} in place of \texttt{left\_\allowbreak{}distrib}) obtained by copying the earlier proof line for line rather than reinventing it.
\end{enumerate}

Two theorems, each reducing a ring-shaped goal to a group-shaped one already solved in Chapter 7, complete the abstract-algebra core of Part II: groups, then rings built on top of them, each with a small library of theorems proved once and inherited by every carrier built so far.
